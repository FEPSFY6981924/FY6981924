Abstract

It is no secret that leaving the Earth’s atmosphere is incredibly dangerous. However, it is equally true that activities in space have vast benefits to the world, from creating hundreds of thousands of jobs, to radically improving cancer treatments. This paper, created by reviewing literature from across the sector, will give a concise analysis and explanation of the risks and benefits associated with carrying out experiments in space, and ends with a discussion and why, despite the risks, we should continue to pursue experiments and further research in this unique microgravity environment.

Introduction

As of 2026, Humanity has not found any trace of life outside our home planet (NASA, 2026). Whilst it appears that other celestial bodies could have the potential to host life (Norris, 2026), these entities are relatively rare in the vast expanse of the universe (Bohl et al., 2026). Although outer space is often barren and hostile, microgravity presents a unique opportunity to conduct experiments that would be impossible on Earth (NASA, 2022)
Many of these experiments have huge benefits to the general public (NASA, 2022), in many different industries (NASA, 2022; Saldana, 2025). Examples of this include medicine and pharmaceuticals (Mulligan, Et al., 2025), material synthesis (ISS National Lab, 2024), and agriculture (NASA, 2025). However, these experiments come with incredible risks and long-term effects on astronauts’ health. (Gray, 2024).
This essay will aim to discuss some of the risks and benefits associated with carrying out research in microgravity, and to impart an understanding of why, despite the dangers, it is crucial that we continue to conduct research in space, for the benefit of humanity as a whole.

Analysis and discussion

1 – The Risks
It is impossible to discuss conducting experiments in space without considering the incredible risks associated with spaceflight and living in space. To ensure safety in spaceflight, and by extension conducting experiments in space, we must work to understand and mitigate these risks. Each country's space agency will have its own methodology for reducing risk (Buchbinder, 2024; ISRO, 2024). Table 1 shows some examples of risks associated with spaceflight.

Table 1: The Risks of Space Exploration

Risk Category Effect of the Risk
Natural causes Risks caused by natural causes include all risks associated with the environment of space, such as space radiation (ESA, 2014), temperature changes (ESA, 2026), meteorites and other natural debris (Riley, 2016), and the vacuum of space (Kolesari and Kindwall, 1982).
These risks can be mitigated with good spacecraft design (Wetherholt and Heimann, 2010), the use of ‘space suits’ (Kenneth and McMann, 2012), and careful risk analysis when planning EVAs (‘spacewalks’) (Belobrajdic, Et al.).
Equipment failure There have been several instances where a failure of equipment or spacecraft has resulted in fatalities (Schmitz et al., 2022). Examples include the space shuttle Challenger in 1986 (NASA, 2023), and the Soyuz 11 in 1971 (Sacco et al., 2024). In the aftermath of these incidents, space agencies across the world conducted investigations and works (Rogers Commission, 1986) to ensure that they would not be repeated. However, it is impossible to completely eradicate the chance of equipment failure occurring, as is also true on Earth.
Human Risk Human risks are caused by the distance between Earth and space (Science Insights Team, 2026), and the effects of microgravity (Tomsia et al., 2024). Examples include mental health issues (Inamdar, 2025), illness (Russomano et al., 2025), muscle atrophy (Science Insights Team, 2026), and injury (Matza, 2026). These risks are especially concerning on long-duration missions (Tran et al., 2021) such as those on the International Space Station (ISS). These risks can be reduced using methods such as journaling and psychological training before launch (Cranford, 2024), ensuring adequate first-aid training (Cassin, 2025) and a thorough examination of astronauts before launch (ESA, 2016).
Risk caused by previous exploration Space exploration often leaves behind “space junk” (Whittaker, 2026): debris ranging in size from microscopic fragments, to full decommissioned satellites (Orbital Radar, 2026). There are thousands of pieces of debris currently in orbit around the planet (Bonnal, 2025) and these pieces have the capacity to do catastrophic harm to astronauts and current spacecraft (Schröder, 2025). Space agencies must track this debris to prevent collisions (Whittaker, 2026).

2 – The benefits
Experiments in space, on the Moon (Callaway, 2026) and on the International Space Station (ISS) have had vast positive impacts for life here on earth, examples including cancer research (ESA, 2024), renewable energy (ESA, 2023), and bone diseases (ESA, 2023). Conducting experiments in space removes the effect of gravity. Whilst this can be achieved on earth using parabolic flight (Villazon, 2021), it can only be used for short experiments. Conducting experiments on space stations such as the ISS allows for analysis over extended periods of time (Gaskill, 2024).
Examples of ISS experiments include the ADSEP-PIL-11, which focusses on growing crystals of medication that could be used to combat cancer (Mulligan, Et al., 2025), and the Osteocytes and Mechano-transduction experiment of 2015, which gave valuable knowledge that could be used to improve treatment for osteoporosis (Creech, 2016).
Outside of the ISS, the European Space Agency (ESA) recently launched the SOLARIS initiative (ESA, 2023) which if successful in its pre-development phase, could result in the development of space-based solar power to provide electricity to Earth (ESA, 2023).
Space exploration also plays a large role in driving innovation in robotics (Jiang et al., 2022), which can then be used in a wide number of capacities, including research (Kazmierczak, 2026), extraterrestrial missions (JAXA, 2026), manufacture (NASA, 2026), and medicine (NHS, 2025).
On top of all this, space is an industry worth over 600 billion USD, (Statista Research Department, 2025) that creates countless opportunities for jobs and education. NASA alone creates or supports 339,645 jobs across the US (Dreier, 2022), whilst the UK space sector employs 55,549 (The London Economics Space Team, 2024).

Conclusion -Why we should conduct these experiments, despite the risks

This paper aimed to provide an understanding of why, despite the unique risks of space, conducting experiments in microgravity is worthwhile and beneficial, and we should continue to conduct these types of experiments in the future. Whilst space experiments come with extraordinary risks, they also give us the opportunity to make discoveries that we would not be able to make on Earth. All of our lives have in some way been improved by developments made in space, whether that be through improved treatments for our loved ones, cleaner energy, or everyday electronics made using robotics pioneered on the ISS.
